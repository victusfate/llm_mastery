# Walk through the experiment

Module 10: Capstone and contribution · Submodule 10-02

[Module overview](../../site/?module=10) · [Complete module reference](../../modules/10-research.md)

1. State a falsifiable hypothesis, baseline, intervention, primary metric, compute ceiling, and failure/stop criteria before running.
2. Reproduce the baseline. If it fails, narrow the question and document the reproduction discrepancy.
3. Run controlled ablations and repeat small configurations across seeds. Add one transfer setting if time allows; otherwise state the limit.
4. Ask a human reviewer or maintainer for feedback through a channel you choose to use. Incorporate substantive critique. The tutor can draft material; it should not contact people without your instruction.
5. Publish a concise report with exact commands, pinned revisions, data retrieval details, raw metric tables, and limitations. Separate unrun design proposals from measured results.
6. Give a 20-minute technical talk and defend implementation details without the tutor. Complete the final practical assessment.

Three-week bootcamp allocation: Week 22 question/baseline; Week 23 intervention/replication; Week 24 review, reproducibility, and defense. The upstream contribution lane starts in Week 1, so the capstone is not your first outside interaction.

## Retrieval and transfer

Close the explanation and state the mechanism in your own words. Name one assumption, one likely implementation failure, and one observation that would reveal it. Then modify a small example and predict the outcome before running. Use the dedicated lab’s completion criteria for practical assessment.

## Spoken transcript

In this section of Capstone and contribution, turn the theory into a bounded experiment. Start with a tiny case and write its expected behavior before running code. Read the complete sequence below, then open the dedicated lab page for the next task. That page contains setup, step-by-step guidance, troubleshooting, and an evidence record. Keep the original baseline and introduce one change at a time. Record exact commands and configurations. If an observation disagrees with your prediction, propose two explanations and design the cheapest test that separates them. The interactive example illustrates a related mechanism; it is not a substitute for running and defending the lab.
